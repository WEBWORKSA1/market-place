Records and Taxes for Online Sellers: What to Track

What online sellers should record, how platform income reporting works in the US, Canada, EU and UK, and how to stay organised before tax season arrives.

By the Market.Place editors·6 min read·Updated 2026-10-31

Selling online creates a paper trail whether you keep one or not. Platforms record every sale, and in many countries they now share seller information with tax authorities. Keeping your own clear records means you can answer questions confidently, claim the costs you are entitled to and avoid surprises. This guide explains what to track and how platform reporting works; it is general information, not tax advice.

Key takeaways

  • Keep records from your first sale: sale price, fees, shipping, cost of goods and receipts.
  • Platforms in the US, Canada, the EU and the UK are required to report certain seller information to tax authorities.
  • A platform report does not automatically mean you owe tax, and not receiving one does not mean you owe nothing.
  • Selling personal items and running a reselling business are often treated differently.
  • Rules change and vary by country, so check official sources and speak with a qualified tax professional.

Why records matter even for casual sellers

Tax authorities increasingly receive data directly from marketplaces and payment platforms. If that data shows sales and you have no records, it becomes harder to show what your actual profit was, or that you sold personal belongings at a loss.

Good records help you:

  • Separate gross sales from actual profit after costs.
  • Show what you originally paid for items, where that matters.
  • Support any deductions or expenses you are entitled to claim.
  • Make business decisions about what is worth selling.
  • Respond quickly if a tax authority asks questions.

What to track

A spreadsheet is enough for most sellers. Accounting software can help once volume grows.

A simple monthly routine

  1. Download the sales and fee reports from each platform you use.
  2. Reconcile them against your spreadsheet and your bank account.
  3. Photograph or scan any paper receipts and store them in a dated folder.
  4. Note any items sold that were personal belongings rather than stock bought to resell.
  5. Back up everything to a second location.

How long you must keep records varies by country, so check the rule that applies where you live. The reselling business guide shows how to combine this with your inventory spreadsheet so you only track things once.

Personal items versus a business

Many tax systems draw a line between occasionally selling your own used belongings and buying or making items to sell for profit. Selling an old sofa for less than you paid is usually viewed differently from buying sofas to flip. The details of where that line sits, and how gains, losses and business income are treated, depend on your country's rules.

Because this distinction matters so much, record which items were personal and, where you can, what you originally paid. If you are unsure which side of the line you fall on, that is a good question to bring to a tax professional.

How platforms report seller income

Several governments now require online marketplaces and payment platforms to collect seller details and report sales information. These are the main regimes buyers and sellers in North America and Europe are likely to encounter.

United States: Form 1099-K

Form 1099-K is an information return that third party settlement organizations, a category that includes payment apps and online marketplaces, and payment card processors file for payees. According to the IRS page Understanding your Form 1099-K, payment apps and online marketplaces must send a 1099-K when payments for goods or services total over $20,000 in more than 200 transactions, while card processors issue one for direct card payments regardless of amount.

That $20,000 and 200-transaction threshold was restored by the One, Big, Beautiful Bill, which reinstated the threshold in effect before the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021, as the IRS explains in its FAQs.

Two points matter most for sellers:

  • The IRS states that whether or not you receive a Form 1099-K, you must still report any income on your tax return.
  • A 1099-K reports gross payments. It does not reflect your costs, fees or whether a personal item sold at a loss, which is why your own records matter.

State tax rules can differ from federal rules, so check those too. See the IRS Form 1099-K FAQs for current details.

Canada: Reporting Rules for Digital Platform Operators

Canada's Reporting Rules for Digital Platforms require reporting platform operators to collect, verify and report information on certain sellers to the Canada Revenue Agency each year. Covered activities include the sale of goods, along with property rentals, personal services and rental of transport.

According to the CRA's guidance, the rules came into force on January 1, 2024, with calendar year 2024 as the first reportable period. The CRA's who is affected page excludes sellers with fewer than 30 sales of goods whose consideration did not exceed $2,800 in the calendar year.

The CRA's tax tip for sellers says platform operators will give reportable sellers an annual copy of the information by January 31, that the CRA may assess a $500 penalty for each failure to provide a tax identification number to the platform, and that sellers who have not reported past platform income can correct returns, including through the Voluntary Disclosures Program.

European Union: DAC7

In the EU, Council Directive (EU) 2021/514, known as DAC7, entered into force on 1 January 2023, according to the European Commission. It places the reporting obligation on platform operators, covers the sale of goods among other activities, and requires annual reporting to the tax authority of the relevant member state. The Commission notes that DAC7 does not change how seller income is taxed under national rules. The full legal text is available on EUR-Lex.

United Kingdom

The UK government's guidance on selling goods or services on a digital platform says new rules started on 1 January 2024, under which platforms report sellers' details and income to HMRC. It lists a small-seller exclusion for those who make fewer than 30 sales of goods in a calendar year and receive less than 2,000 euros (about £1,700) for those sales. HMRC also states that being reported does not automatically mean you owe tax.

Sales tax, VAT and other obligations

Income tax is not the only consideration. Depending on where you sell and ship, sales tax, VAT or GST may apply. In many places, large marketplaces collect and remit these taxes on certain sales, but that does not necessarily cover every sale or every obligation, especially if you also sell through your own website. Business registration and licensing rules may apply too. Check your tax authority's guidance or ask a professional.

Preparing for tax season

  1. Gather platform reports and any information returns you received, such as a 1099-K or a platform's annual seller statement.
  2. Compare them with your records. Differences often come from refunds, shipping charged to buyers or fees.
  3. Separate personal sales from business sales with your notes on original cost.
  4. Total your costs by category.
  5. Note anything unusual, like a large one-off sale or a platform report you think is wrong.
  6. Book time with a tax professional if your situation has changed, you started reselling, or you are unsure how the rules apply.

A final word

This guide is general information to help you get organised. It is not tax, legal or accounting advice, and the rules described here change and have conditions not covered in full. Always confirm current rules on your tax authority's website and speak with a qualified tax professional before making decisions. Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay tax on things I sell online?

It depends on where you live, whether you are selling personal items or running a business, and whether you sold at a gain. Platform reporting does not by itself mean you owe tax, so check your tax authority's guidance and speak with a tax professional about your situation.

What is Form 1099-K?

It is a US information return that payment apps, online marketplaces and card processors file to report payments they processed for you. The IRS says you must report income on your return whether or not you receive one.

Will a marketplace report my sales to the tax authority if I live outside the US?

In many countries, yes. Canada, EU member states and the UK all have rules requiring certain digital platforms to collect seller information and report it to the tax authority, with exclusions for some small sellers.

What records should I keep if I only sell occasionally?

Keep the platform's sales reports, proof of what you originally paid for items where you can, and receipts for fees, shipping and supplies. Even occasional sellers benefit from a simple spreadsheet in case a tax authority asks questions.
