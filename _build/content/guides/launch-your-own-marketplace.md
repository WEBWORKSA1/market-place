Running a marketplace means you are not just selling. You are building a place where other people buy and sell, which makes it one of the hardest and most rewarding online businesses to start. This guide covers the decisions that matter most, from choosing a niche to launch day, so you can test your idea without overbuilding.

## Key takeaways

- Start with a narrow niche where existing options are clearly underserving buyers or sellers.
- Solve the chicken-and-egg problem by seeding supply first, often by hand.
- Liquidity, meaning listings that reliably turn into transactions, matters more than growth in sign-ups.
- Choose a revenue model and take rate that reflect the value you add and stay competitive.
- Treat trust and safety, payments and legal basics as core product, not afterthoughts.

## Choose a niche you can win

General marketplaces already exist and are very hard to compete with. New marketplaces usually succeed by doing one thing much better for a specific group.

Good marketplace niches tend to have:

- **Fragmented supply:** many small sellers who struggle to reach buyers alone.
- **Fragmented demand:** many buyers who find it hard to locate the right seller.
- **A gap in existing platforms:** poor search for the category, weak trust tools, missing features or high friction.
- **Repeat transactions:** buyers who come back, which makes acquisition costs worthwhile.
- **Something you understand:** insider knowledge helps you recruit sellers and design features they need.

Validate before you build. Talk to 20 or more potential sellers and buyers. Ask how they buy and sell today, what frustrates them and what they would pay to solve it. If you cannot find sellers willing to list for free during a pilot, the idea needs work.

## Solve the chicken-and-egg problem

Buyers come where there is selection; sellers come where there are buyers. Every marketplace faces this loop. The usual answer is to break it deliberately on one side first.

### Tactics for seeding supply

1. **Recruit sellers by hand.** Message, call or visit them. Early marketplaces are built one relationship at a time.
2. **Offer a reason to list now,** such as free listing, reduced commission for founding sellers, or a done-for-you listing service.
3. **Add value even without buyers,** for example a storefront, booking tool or inventory page sellers can share with their own customers.
4. **Constrain geography or category** so a small number of sellers feels like real choice.
5. **Aggregate public information** where permitted, but respect terms of use, copyright and privacy rules.

### Bringing in the first buyers

- Go where your buyers already gather: communities, forums, events and social channels relevant to the niche.
- Ask founding sellers to share their storefronts.
- Write useful content, like guides and comparisons, that answers questions your buyers search for.
- Follow up personally after early transactions to learn what worked and what did not.

## Reach liquidity before scaling

Liquidity means a buyer can reliably find what they need and a seller can reliably sell. A marketplace with thousands of sign-ups and few completed transactions is not working yet.

Track a few signals:

| Signal | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Share of listings that sell within a set period | Whether supply matches demand |
| Share of searches that lead to a contact or purchase | Whether buyers find what they want |
| Repeat buyers and repeat sellers | Whether the experience is worth returning to |
| Time from listing to first inquiry | How active demand is |
| Transactions that move off-platform | Whether you are providing enough value |

If liquidity is weak, narrow your focus further rather than broadening it. It is easier to be excellent in one city or one category, then expand.

## Set your revenue model and take rate

A take rate is the share of each transaction the marketplace keeps. It is the most common model, but not the only one.

- **Commission on transactions:** aligns your income with seller success. Works when you facilitate payment.
- **Listing fees:** simple, but can discourage sellers from listing.
- **Seller subscriptions:** predictable income, useful for professional sellers.
- **Featured placement or promotion:** sellers pay for visibility.
- **Lead fees:** sellers pay for qualified inquiries, common for services.

When setting a take rate, consider the value you add, such as demand, payments, trust and tools, along with what competitors and alternatives cost. If your take rate is too high relative to that value, buyers and sellers will connect once and then transact privately. Building real value into the transaction, like payment protection, reviews, messaging and scheduling, helps keep them on the platform.

## Build trust and safety from day one

People will only transact with strangers if they feel safe. As the operator, you are responsible for creating that safety.

**Core trust and safety features:**

- Clear rules on prohibited items and behaviour.
- Seller verification appropriate to your category and legal obligations.
- Reviews and ratings tied to completed transactions.
- In-platform messaging and payments, so you can detect fraud and resolve disputes.
- A reporting tool and a clear process for handling reports.
- A documented dispute and refund process.

Expect scams. Fake payment notices, off-platform payment requests and account takeovers, which we cover in [how sellers avoid scams](../guides/avoid-seller-scams.html) and [how buyers avoid scams](../guides/avoid-buyer-scams.html), will target your users too. Design your product so the safe path is also the easiest path.

## Choose your technology

### No-code and SaaS marketplace platforms

Several platforms let you launch a marketplace with listings, search, user profiles, messaging and payments, without writing code.

**Good for:** testing demand quickly, limited budgets, standard marketplace flows.
**Watch for:** limits on customisation, transaction or subscription costs, and how easily you can export your data later.

### Custom development

Building your own marketplace gives full control over features and experience.

**Good for:** unusual transaction flows, complex pricing or scheduling, and scale once demand is proven.
**Watch for:** higher cost, longer time to launch, and ongoing maintenance and security responsibilities.

A common path is to launch on a no-code platform, prove liquidity and then rebuild custom features where the tool holds you back.

### Questions to ask before choosing a tool

1. Can it handle your core transaction, such as one-off sales, bookings, rentals or quotes?
2. Does it support marketplace payments and seller payouts in your countries and currencies?
3. Can you export listings, users and transaction data if you move later?
4. What does it cost as you grow, including any per-transaction charges?
5. How does it handle search, reviews, messaging and moderation tools?

### Payments

Use a payment provider that supports marketplaces, with features for onboarding sellers, splitting payments and paying them out. In many jurisdictions, holding and moving money on behalf of others is regulated, so avoid building your own system for collecting and redistributing funds without legal advice.

## Cover the legal basics

Get professional advice for your jurisdiction, but plan for at least:

- **Terms of service** for buyers and sellers, including fees, prohibited items, disputes and account suspension.
- **A privacy policy** that reflects how you collect and use personal data, and compliance with applicable data protection laws.
- **Seller verification obligations.** In the EU, for example, the Digital Services Act requires online marketplaces to ensure that [sellers give verified information on their identity before they can start selling](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/dsa-impact-platforms), according to the European Commission.
- **Tax reporting.** Platform operators in several regions must collect and report seller information to tax authorities. Our [records and taxes guide](../guides/seller-taxes-records.html) summarises rules in the US, Canada, the EU and the UK.
- **Category-specific rules** for regulated goods or services such as food, alcohol, vehicles, financial products or childcare.

## A 90-day launch plan

1. **Weeks 1 to 2:** interview buyers and sellers; define your niche and core transaction.
2. **Weeks 3 to 4:** choose your platform, set up payments, and draft terms and policies with advice.
3. **Weeks 5 to 8:** recruit and onboard founding sellers; help them create strong listings using guidance like [writing listings that sell](../guides/write-listings-that-sell.html).
4. **Weeks 9 to 10:** soft launch to a small group of buyers; watch every transaction closely.
5. **Weeks 11 to 12:** fix the biggest problems, gather reviews and testimonials, and open more widely.
6. **Ongoing:** measure liquidity, talk to users weekly and expand only once the core works.
