Photos are the first thing a buyer judges and often the only thing they look at before deciding whether to read on. The good news is that a modern phone is more than capable of producing listing photos that look trustworthy and professional. What makes the difference is light, background, angles and honesty, not equipment.

## Key takeaways

- Soft, indirect daylight from a window beats almost any cheap artificial light.
- Shoot on a plain, uncluttered background so the item is the only thing the eye sees.
- Follow a consistent shot list: hero shot, all sides, labels, details and every flaw.
- Clean the item and the lens before you shoot; both make a visible difference.
- Edit lightly for brightness and cropping, never to hide damage or change colour.

## Light: the single biggest upgrade

Most bad listing photos are bad because of light. Overhead room lights cast yellow tones and hard shadows, and the phone compensates with grainy, smeared images.

Set up next to a large window during the day. Indirect light, meaning daylight that is not hitting the item directly in a harsh beam, is ideal. If the sun is streaming in, hang a thin white sheet or sheer curtain over the window to soften it.

Two cheap tricks help a lot:

- **A white reflector.** Prop a piece of white card or foam board on the side of the item opposite the window. It bounces light back into the shadows.
- **Turn off room lights.** Mixing warm bulbs with cool daylight produces odd colour casts. Use one light source.

If you must shoot at night, use one or two daylight-balanced bulbs from the same direction, diffused through paper or fabric, rather than the ceiling light.

## Background and setup

The background should disappear. A plain wall, a sheet of white or light grey poster board, or a clean wooden table all work. Curve a large sheet of card from the wall down onto the table to create a seamless backdrop with no visible horizon line.

Clear the frame of everything except the item. Pets, laundry, cables and other listings in the background make buyers wonder about the item's condition and history.

For clothing, choose between a flat lay on a plain surface, a hanger against a clean wall, or a mannequin or dress form. Pick one style and use it consistently across your shop so your listings look organised.

## The shot list buyers expect

A consistent shot list means you never forget an angle and buyers find what they need quickly. Adjust it by category, but this covers most items.

1. **Hero shot.** The whole item, front on, filling most of the frame. This is your thumbnail, so make it the cleanest and brightest photo.
2. **Back and sides.** Every surface a buyer might care about.
3. **Top and bottom.** Especially for shoes, furniture, bags and electronics.
4. **Brand and labels.** Logos, size tags, care labels and material content.
5. **Model or serial information.** For electronics and appliances, a readable shot of the model plate or settings screen showing it powers on.
6. **Details.** Hardware, stitching, texture, ports, buttons.
7. **Flaws.** Every scratch, stain, chip or missing piece, up close and in good light.
8. **Scale.** A photo with a common object or a tape measure, or a shot of the item being worn or used, so buyers understand size.
9. **Everything included.** Accessories, cables, box and manuals laid out together.

### Getting the thumbnail right

In search results, buyers see one small image next to dozens of others. That thumbnail decides whether they tap. Make it the clearest, brightest photo of the whole item, centred, with a little space around the edges so nothing is cropped when the platform resizes it. Avoid text overlays, collages and borders; many platforms discourage them, and they make the item itself harder to see at small sizes.

Before uploading, view your hero shot at thumbnail size on your phone. If you cannot tell what the item is in a second, reshoot it closer or against a plainer background.

### Photo order tells a story

Order your photos the way a buyer would inspect the item in person: the whole thing first, then each side, then details, then flaws, then everything included. Put flaw photos in the main set, not hidden at the end after unrelated shots. Buyers who find flaws easily trust the rest of your listing more.

### Category tweaks

| Category | Extra shots worth taking |
|---|---|
| Clothing | Measurement shots with a tape (pit to pit, length), fabric close-up, inside seams |
| Shoes | Soles, heels, insoles, size tag inside the tongue |
| Electronics | Device powered on, battery or storage screen, all ports |
| Furniture | Shot in a room for scale, joints, underside, any wobble or repair |
| Collectibles | Edition marks, signatures, certificates, packaging corners |
| Jewellery | Clasp, hallmarks, close-up of stones, item on a hand or neck for scale |

## Phone camera settings and technique

You do not need manual controls, but a few habits help.

- **Clean the lens.** Phone lenses collect fingerprints and pocket lint. A wipe with a soft cloth fixes a surprising number of hazy photos.
- **Use the main lens.** Many phones have several cameras. The standard lens usually gives the sharpest, most natural result. Avoid digital zoom; move closer instead.
- **Tap to focus and adjust exposure.** Tap the item on screen to focus. Most phones let you slide exposure up or down after tapping. Brighten slightly for white backgrounds.
- **Hold steady.** Brace your elbows on the table or use a cheap phone tripod. Sharpness matters most on detail and flaw shots.
- **Shoot at the item's level.** For most objects, hold the camera roughly level with the centre of the item rather than shooting down from above, which distorts proportions. Flat lays are the exception.
- **Skip portrait mode for listings.** Background blur can look stylish but may hide edges and details buyers want to see.
- **Avoid flash.** Direct flash creates harsh reflections and flattens texture. Use it only to reveal something dark, like a serial number inside a cavity.

## Shooting shiny, dark and tricky items

Some items fight back.

**Shiny surfaces** such as glass, chrome or glossy plastic reflect everything, including you. Shoot at a slight angle, use a large soft light source, and surround the item with white card so reflections are clean and plain.

**Dark items** lose detail. Use more light than you think you need, a lighter background for contrast, and increase exposure slightly.

**White items** on a white background can disappear. Use a light grey or contrasting background instead.

**Small items** benefit from shooting in a small, white space, such as a box lined with paper, with light from one side and a reflector on the other.

## Editing without misleading

Light editing makes photos clearer. Heavy editing makes them dishonest, and it leads to returns.

Good edits:

- Crop and straighten.
- Brighten slightly if the photo is underexposed.
- Correct white balance so whites look white.

Edits to avoid:

- Filters that change colour or saturation.
- Smoothing or retouching that removes wear.
- Compositing or replacing the item with a stock image.

Check the edited photo against the real item in daylight. If the colour on screen does not match what you see, the buyer will notice too. Mention in the description if a colour is hard to capture accurately.

## A repeatable photo session checklist

Batching saves time. Before you start, set up once and shoot several items in a row.

1. Set up next to a window with a plain backdrop and a white reflector.
2. Turn off overhead lights.
3. Clean each item: lint roller for clothing, microfibre cloth for electronics.
4. Wipe your phone lens.
5. Shoot your full shot list for item one, then move to the next item without changing the setup.
6. Take one extra photo of any tag or note to identify each item, so photos do not get mixed up.
7. Review on a larger screen if possible and reshoot anything blurry or dark.
8. Make light edits, then upload.
